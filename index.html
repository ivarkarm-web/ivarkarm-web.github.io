How I think about this

Manifesto

For me the music is the journey, not the product.

The street is my stage because it is real — no tickets, no barriers. I was terrible when I started, so I played where somebody would pay me to leave. Then I got better. No commissioned stage has ever given me what a kid stopping in awe on a corner gives me. If you stop, you are part of it.

Chapter 01 · Early Life

The Teacher's Weird Kid

Eastern Estonia, early nineties

I grew up in eastern Estonia in the early nineties, right after the Soviet Union collapsed and the country had to figure out what it was. My mom taught Estonian to Russian kids, edited the local paper and translated on the side, so she was always busy and my sister and I mostly entertained ourselves. I was a restless kid who couldn't sit still. I pushed my mom's nerves right to the last point, but never over it, and never out of malice. Mostly boredom.

Pots, pans and a harmonica

Music started like it does for every kid: pots, pans, whatever utensils I could find. Then a harmonica, which I played over a Christmas reggae album we had at home. My mom was not a fan of the harmonica. My parents loved music but neither of them played anything. In Soviet times they listened to Finnish radio and taped over whatever came through, Queen, the Stones. My dad was a huge Queen fan and kept guitars around the house that nobody wanted him to play.

Skipping school

School and I didn't get along. I skipped it to wander through abandoned Soviet factories, walk the Baltic beach alone and skate. I hitchhiked alone for the first time at ten, and properly from thirteen. I knew I wasn't going to make it on school, so I'd have to find some other way to survive in this world. I had no idea what. I daydreamed about a one-way ticket out of that eastern Estonian slum, to find some kind of meaning out in the wide world.

The weirdo with the djembe

By my teens I had dreadlocks, a skateboard, a beanie and a small African djembe my aunt got me at a discount from the Viljandi folk festival. The skin was slightly torn, but it held and it played fine. There was nobody like me in town, so I walked around with dreads and a drum, played along to the jocks' speakers and got known as the weirdo son of the Estonian teacher. That meant a lot of fights, because whenever someone got a bad grade I was the one who had to answer for it. I bounced between schools without ever finding a circle of friends.

In the end I was sent to home schooling instead. I'd finish my homework and browse YouTube, and that's where I found handpan videos. It took me years to get one. But that was the seed.

The guitar I couldn't fix

At home there was my grandfather's old seven-string guitar. In woodworking class I tried to turn it into a six-string. I'm no carpenter, so I failed miserably, and it ended up lying around the house. Now and then I'd put on guitar music and pretend I knew where the notes were. I had no idea.

Chapter 02 · Way to Music

From a one-way ticket to a €30 drum

Barcelona, with one word of Spanish

At 17–18 I was in culinary school in Tallinn, with a full-time job in the old town and my own rented apartment. It was a dull life and I hated the restaurant industry, even though I kept telling myself I liked it because I had bills to pay. After about a year, when I turned 18, I got a passport, took my last month's salary, paid my rent and bought a one-way ticket to Barcelona with no idea what I'd do there. I just knew I couldn't keep going from work to home and back with nothing to look forward to.

I landed, took the bus to the city center, and the only Spanish word I knew was "okupa." I spent the first night on a hotel terrace and woke up when a waiter was setting the tables. I was a bit ashamed, because it was the first time I'd been homeless. Then I started walking around asking random people "okupa? okupa?" and waving my arms. Believe it or not, half a day later somebody took me to one. The people who opened the door were Estonians.

Eight months, no plan

They let me in. I was just a kid and it was awkward. We chatted, they told me a bit about their life there and offered me a room that was full of junk. Instead of staying, I met two other travelers, a Bulgarian and a Hungarian, and we drifted around Spain toward Valencia and the villages nearby. We went to demonstrations, slept in abandoned houses, tried to find work picking oranges. It was the time of the Arab Spring and the 15M movement, and in Valencia I took part in the occupation of the town square. Then they both ditched me and I found my way back to Barcelona alone.

After that I hitchhiked across Europe alone for the first time. It was around seven or eight months in total, April to October. I ended up in Paris, where a city council worker took me in for two weeks, looked after me and put me on a plane home to Estonia.

Polymer: the double life

I moved into Polymer Culture Factory in Tallinn, an enormous abandoned toy factory that rented rooms to artists. The whole building was a gallery and a pile of art projects: music, drum workshops, a community kitchen, a rooftop garden. I was living a double life. In one, I put on white gloves at a hotel, straightened my back and said "yes sir, no sir." In the other, I was a free-floating creative soul in a factory full of people like me. When I fully embraced the second one, I quit the hotel and flew to Berlin on another one-way ticket.

Berlin and the €30 djembe

In Berlin I ran into the same Estonians who'd welcomed me in Barcelona, completely by accident. They'd moved on to squatting and taught me how to survive the streets: where to get food, where to get clothes, where to sleep. Eventually I drifted away from them. I didn't fancy sitting around all day drinking beer and asking for money.

One night I found a broken two-sided wooden drum in the trash and started busking with it. Not much money, but enough for a döner. At Rosenthaler Platz I met two Latvian guys, one on djembe and one on darbuka, and joined their jam. The djembe player was a god to me. Until then percussion was something I did on the side, but watching him play like a virtuoso with crazy technique, I decided to commit to it full time. I sold some clothes in the park to afford a better drum: a €30 djembe from a Kreuzberg thrift shop full of vintage jackets, military boots and used accordions. It sounded earthy and a bit rough, and it changed my life.

I was terrible at first. So I came up with a system: on weekend mornings I'd go to hostel entrances where people were hungover and play as loud and as badly as I could until someone gave me 10 or 20 euros to go away. I made about 60–70 a weekend. I knew I sucked, but I had to figure out how to get paid for it. The rest of the week I practiced in the underground, six to nine hours in a row, for a year or two.

The handpan

I went back to Estonia to renew my documents and got pulled into the restaurant rat race again. But by then I'd seen the handpan on YouTube and I knew it was my next step. I'd always liked melody, and a djembe can only go so far. I cleaned tables and hauled restaurant equipment for catering companies, lived on my sister's couch and saved. When I had 1.5k, I ordered my first handpan: a C major pentatonic Spacedrum, the one everybody wanted back then because of Yuki Koshimoto. I played it like I played the djembe, six to nine hours a day when I could.

At first I kept the catering job and busked in my free time. Then I dropped the catering and committed completely. My coworkers made fun of it, but a few years later some of them were catering events where I was the one performing.

The drummer boy with the dog

I spent two or three years practicing in Tallinn. I got a small name as "the drummer boy with the dog": Moko, a Jack Russell I'd brought from Berlin, who'd hang out in my handpan case while I busked, wander around the old town and come back every now and then.

Then I got drafted. I realized fast that I didn't want to be part of the war machine, found a way out, and they kicked me out right after the first snow. I had nowhere to go, so I crashed in a friend's attic in the old town for a month. It was freezing. I left the dog with my sister and took a bus to Berlin, because even a Berlin winter isn't as cold as Tallinn. I lived in a house project there for a bit under half a year, then went on through the Netherlands and Belgium before coming back to Estonia again.

The morning the handpan was gone

After that trip I had nowhere to live again and was crashing in the same attic, keeping my handpan in the locked staircase. One morning I got up, got dressed to go busking, and my handpan wasn't there. It had been stolen. That was the lowest moment I've had. But I got my shit together, pulled out my djembe and went busking anyway.

Then I started a crowdfunding campaign to replace my instruments. In The Hague I'd once had a short walk and talk with the maker of Godan, so it came as a total shock when he wrote and said he wanted to sponsor me with a brand new handpan, free of charge. It's the nicest thing anyone has ever done for me, to this day.

Chapter 03 · Street Stories

What actually happened out there

“
Warschauer Straße: the Hulk

One Friday night I was playing djembe on Warschauer Straße when a man in his underwear ran up and insisted I paint him green like the Hulk, with the spray paint he'd brought. You don't have to ask me twice. I did it. For the rest of the night he roamed the street as the Hulk. I never found out what happened to him. This was the early 2010s, and crazy shit happened on that street.

“
Poland: pancakes and aliens

I was stranded on the side of a road in Poland when a truck driver picked me up and took me to his parents' little farm. They fed me, I got loads of vodka, and his father talked to me about aliens and space. In the morning his mother made pancakes, packed them for the road and gave me a bottle of fresh milk. Then her son drove me to the border to Germany.

Paris: two weeks

I'd hitchhiked across Europe alone for the first time and ended up in Paris with nothing. A city council worker took me in, looked after me for two weeks and put me on a plane home. I never really got to thank him properly.

The notes people leave me

When artists draw me, or people drop little notes of appreciation in my case, it hits hard. The latest one:

“I see you very often on Ermou Street and you're my favorite street artist. If you ever need assistance with your dog, you can call me. I'm a freelance dog groomer and trainer, of course free of charge. I also wanted to ask if you teach handpan, I'm interested just for my leisure time. Thanks for making art, keep going.”

Samos, then Athens

When I was offered the chance to move to Greece, I took it, because I felt trapped in Estonia. It put me somewhere new, where I could start with a fresh mindset.

On Samos I opened a restaurant with my former employer. We had our differences, and after a bit under a year I left for Athens and picked up my street-artist life again.

The dogs came too. Three generations, all from Berlin: Moko, who left puppies in Estonia; Chris, the one I kept; and Chris's daughter Bella, born in Samos. Moko passed away a few years ago.

Chapter 05 · Instruments

What's in the bag

◎
Centerpiece · Steel

Handpan

My centerpiece. My first was a C major pentatonic Spacedrum, the one everybody wanted because of Yuki Koshimoto. Years later I learned it sounded closer to a steelpan than what we call a handpan today. I loved her more than anything anyway.

◯
Early road · Skin

Djembe

The first one was my aunt's torn-skin gift. The one that changed my life cost €30 in a Berlin thrift store full of vintage jackets, military boots and used accordions. It sounded earthy and a bit rough, and it was a huge step up from the soaked, rotten drum I found in the trash.

筝

Koto

It fell into my lap. A friend needed a new home for his cat, a girl who worked at a bar took it, and when we delivered the cat I saw a koto at her place and asked for it right there. She said no. A year later she was short on cash and sold it to me, and I paid in bitcoin. I didn't look up anything about it. I tried random tunings until I found ones I liked, started busking with it, and played my first paid gig on it a couple of months later.

🎶

Harmonica

Where it all started, over a Christmas reggae record. My mom hated it.

🥁

Everything else

Cajón, jaw harps, keyboards, whatever's lying around. I improvise on all of it until I'm happy with the result. I don't really do finished pieces. For me the music is the journey, not the product.

Chapter 07 · Road Notes

Things I figured out out here

Mostly the hard way.

  • The street performers come and go, and it's your job to appreciate the right ones.
  • Most of the people who changed my life never left me a phone number.
  • I was terrible when I started, so I played where somebody would pay me to leave. Then I got better.
  • No commissioned stage has ever given me what a kid stopping in awe on a street corner gives me.
  • A busker can pack up when he wants and take a day off when he wants, as long as he's disciplined enough not to starve.
  • For me the music is the journey, not the product. That's why you won't find a polished album.
  • The street doesn't pay on time and it doesn't pay the same twice, but nobody tells you when to stop.
